The client-eye checks run on the panel's server, which is not inside the country where your users are, so they cannot see a block there. Five sources look from inside one country (Russia by default) instead: [Globalping](#globalping), the [block lists](#block-lists), the [TSPU probe](#tspu-probe), [cheburcheck](#cheburcheck) and [bsbord](#bsbord). Each is an integration of its own and **off by default**. The panel [combines what they see](#how-the-sources-join-the-verdict) into one verdict per node, keeps a week of it, and raises an [alert](#the-alert) when a node stops opening. The [kl!ck reports](#klck-reports) are shown next to them and do not change the verdict.

## In the admin

**Reachability** is a section of its own in the menu, right after **Health** (on a phone, under **More**). Everyone who can open Health can open it; the sources and the country are the owner's, and changing a source asks for a fresh confirmation.

- **Header**: the country the sources look from; the owner picks it here. The country (default `RU`) is one shared setting that every source looks from. The block lists, the TSPU probe, cheburcheck and bsbord exist for Russia only. Links next to it jump down to *Nodes*, *Events* and *Sources*.
- **Summary**: how many nodes are reachable, TCP blocked, unreachable, listed (in a block list, not checked live) or without data, in the colours and marks of Health. With every source off it says so instead and points to the sources. The page also names a network that reaches none of your nodes: no alert covers that, because the network cuts every node alike.
- **Nodes**: for each node its verdict now, with the same tooltip as on Health, then a line per source (*Globalping*, *Lists*, *Probe*, *cheburcheck*, *Own probe*, *bsbord*): what that source says now and a lane of its verdicts over 7 days. A lane is a strip of segments coloured by verdict; hover or focus a segment to see its verdict and when it started and ended, and move along a lane with the arrow keys. A source that is off is dimmed and its lanes are empty. On a screen narrower than 1280 pixels each node is a card with the lanes under its verdict. While Globalping checks IPv6 too, the verdict comes as an *IPv4* and an *IPv6* pill and Globalping has an IPv6 lane: see [IPv6](#ipv6).
- **Recent events**: the `country_blocked` alerts of the last 7 days, open and closed, each a link to the alert on **Health → Alerts**.
- **Sources**: a list of [Globalping](#globalping), the [block lists](#block-lists), the [TSPU probe](#tspu-probe), [cheburcheck](#cheburcheck), [bsbord](#bsbord) and the [kl!ck reports](#klck-reports), each with what it is (free or paid), where it looks from (*Russia only* or *any country*) and its state in a word (*Works*, *Off*, *Error*, *Needs setup*). Pick one to open it: its switch and state, then its settings in blocks (*Connection*, *Checks*, *Budget*), what leaves the panel, and *Test* and *Disconnect*. On a wide screen the list stays on the left and the source opens on the right; on a narrower one the list is a strip of tiles on top; on a phone a tap opens the source and *All sources* leads back. The arrow keys, Home and End walk the list, Enter opens. The open source is in the address (`?source=bsbord`), so a link or a reload keeps it; without it the first source that is on opens, else Globalping.

**Health → Checks** still shows the verdict as its last column, *From Russia* (a row per node on a phone), and the node page as a row of its client-eye checks. Both are hidden while every source is off, and their titles lead to this section. The API has the week as `CountryReachabilityService.GetReachabilityHistory`: at most 7 days, readable like `GetChecks` by every role and by API tokens.

## Globalping

The first source asks probes inside the country (home and mobile networks first) to ping every online node and to open TCP connections to its ports, and compares the answers. It uses [Globalping](https://globalping.io), a free network of probes. Switching it on sends the IPv4 address of each online node to Globalping and to nobody else; with the IPv6 switch on (the default), also the IPv6 address of each node whose IPv6 works.

- **Where**: the *Globalping* card in **Reachability → Sources**.
- **Settings** (owner only): on or off, the interval in minutes (default 15, at least 10), the IPv6 switch (default on: a node whose IPv6 works is also measured by its IPv6 address, in a round of its own with a control site reached over IPv6, so a probe without IPv6 is never counted against it; both rounds share the budget below, so without a token the rounds come a little less often) and an optional token, which is write-only and raises the free limit from 250 to 500 tests an hour. **Test** reads Globalping's rate-limit endpoint and sends no address. **Disconnect** switches the integration off and forgets the token.
- **Off means off.** With the integration off no round starts and nothing is sent. A round in flight is cancelled when you switch it off. Stored results stay for 7 days, but the API stops returning them.
- **What is asked.** Three probes (two when the budget needs it) take part in every round; call the number K. For Russia, up to K-1 of them are named operators and one is a random home or mobile probe, which finds the networks the panel does not name. The named operators are MTS, Beeline, MegaFon, Rostelecom, T2 (Tele2) and ER-Telecom, each by the AS numbers of its RIPE organisation. Other countries have no list: all their probes are random, as before. Each node gets an ICMP ping of its address and a TCP ping of each TCP port of its active profiles (VLESS, and the HTTPS site of Hysteria2). A node with only UDP profiles gets the ICMP ping alone: Globalping has no UDP test.
- **Which operators.** Once per round the panel reads Globalping's list of online probes (`/v1/probes`: free, and not counted against the limit) and picks one home or mobile probe on the networks of each operator it wants. The order: first the dark probes of the newest `partial` round (see below), asked again on the same network and in the same city; then the operators by rotation, one that was never asked first, then the one asked longest ago. With four operators online, each is asked at least every second round at K=3 and every fourth at K=2 (30 minutes and 1 hour at the default interval). A network that is asked again takes a named place, so while it stays dark the others rotate more slowly. An operator counts as asked once its probe is picked, even if that probe then runs no test.
- **No probe now.** An operator with no home or mobile probe online is skipped before asking, which costs nothing, and is listed as `no_probe` on the round: small operators often have none. If the list cannot be read, the round runs as it did before the operators: one random location, `no_probe` empty.
- **Control.** Every round the same probes also open TCP 443 to a large site that is never blocked in that country (`ya.ru` for `RU`). A probe that cannot reach it is thrown away for that round. With fewer than two valid probes the verdict is `unknown`.
- **Verdict per node.** `ok`: TCP connects from at least half of the valid probes (ICMP for a node without TCP ports). `tcp_blocked`: ICMP answers from at least half, but TCP fails from all of them: the connection requests are dropped while the address is not. `blocked`: nothing passes. `partial`: see the next point. `unknown`: too few probes, a limit, an error, a timeout, or a split picture that proves nothing.
- **`partial` and dark probes.** A round is `partial` for a node when it would be `ok` (or a split picture), but a home or mobile probe gets nothing from the node, not even a ping, although in the same round it reached another of your nodes, and another probe did reach this one. Such a probe is **dark** for the node: one network is cut off while the rest see it, which a majority vote alone would call `ok`. A probe is dark only when it finished every test of the node: a timeout or a failure on the probe's side never makes it dark. `tcp_blocked`, `blocked` and `unknown` from too few probes stay as they are.
- **Where `partial` cannot happen.** A fleet of one node never gets it: nothing else proves that the probe works. A network that drops ping cannot make a node with no TCP ports `partial`: for such a node a probe counts as having reached another node only by ping, and a probe without ping answers is not dark. A round that fell back to data-centre probes (fewer than two valid home or mobile ones) is never `partial`. At K=2 the random probe can land on the named one; the round then has a single probe and runs again across any network, data centres included, which happened in about 1 of 6 such rounds in the probe list of October 2026. K=3 avoids most of it.
- **Budget.** A round costs (1 control + for each node 1 + its TCP ports) x probes tests. If the rounds of an hour would use more than 80 % of the limit, the probes go down to two, and if that is still too much the interval is stretched (the panel logs it once). On a rate limit the round is `unknown` and the next one waits until the limit resets. The named probes are among those probes and the probe list is free, so this formula holds as it is.
- **Both editions.** The VPS panel looks every 30 seconds whether a round is due; the Cloudflare edition does it from its once-a-minute cron.

## Block lists

The second source of the country check: the panel compares the IPv4 address of every active node with public lists of blocked addresses. The lists are downloaded to the panel and compared there, so no node address leaves the panel. It is an integration of its own, **off by default**, and works for Russia only: with another country selected the card says so and nothing is downloaded.

- **Where**: the *Block lists* card in **Reachability → Sources**. Its verdicts join the node's verdict there, in the *From Russia* column and in the node row.
- **The lists.** [`ipsum.lst`](https://antifilter.download/list/ipsum.lst) from antifilter.download, the RKN registry summed up to /24 subnets, with [antifilter.network](https://antifilter.network/download/ipsum.lst) as the mirror when the main address does not answer. [`community.lst`](https://community.antifilter.download/list/community.lst), addresses the community found blocked. [cdn-ip-ranges](https://github.com/123jjck/cdn-ip-ranges), the ranges of CDN and hosting providers: it only names the subnet a node sits in ("Hetzner subnet, DE") and never makes a verdict. IPv6 addresses are not checked.
- **Schedule.** Every 6 hours the panel refreshes the lists with a conditional request (an unchanged list costs a 304) and compares every node again; a new node is compared at once. The lists are kept in memory only. When no list can be downloaded, the next try is in 15 minutes. **Test** does it now and reports the date of the lists, the number of prefixes and CDN ranges, how many nodes are listed, and whether the mirror answered. **Disconnect** switches it off and also deletes the downloaded lists and every stored result.
- **`listed` never opens an alert.** A node whose address is in a list and that no live source has checked shows `listed`, in yellow: in the registry, not checked live. The registry says the address is written down, not that its traffic is dropped, and a list alone would raise alerts for every node in a crowded subnet. A live verdict from Globalping always wins; the lists then add their detail to its tooltip.
- **Off means off.** With the source off nothing is downloaded, neither on schedule nor by **Test**, and Health shows nothing from it. Stored results stay hidden until they expire after 7 days.

## TSPU probe

The third source of the country check, and the one that tests the TCP ports of your nodes from inside the country with your own hardware. One of your nodes in the country, the **probe node**, finds the box of the national DPI (the TSPU) on its own path and then sends TCP SYN packets with a limited TTL to the other nodes: when the packets die silently at that box and no router behind it answers, the port is blocked. The method is the one of [tspu-check](https://github.com/porutchik/tspu-check) and [cheburcheck](https://github.com/LowderPlay/cheburcheck); the node agent re-implements it and uses none of their code. It is an integration of its own, **off by default**, and works for Russia only: with another country selected the card says so and nothing is sent.

- **Where**: the *TSPU probe* card in **Reachability → Sources**. Its verdicts join the node's verdict there, in the *From Russia* column and in the node row; the tooltip names the probe node and the hop where the box sits (`dpi_hop`).
- **Settings** (owner only; changing them asks for a fresh confirmation): on or off, the **probe node** and the interval (default 120 minutes, at least 30). The picker lists the nodes in the selected country first. A node can be the probe node when it is online and its agent declares `country-probe/1`; an older agent has to be updated first. Nothing runs until a probe node is chosen.
- **A data-centre view.** The probe node sits in a data centre, so it sees the filtering of its own uplink, not what a person on a home or mobile network sees. Its verdicts carry the confidence `dc_only`, and the home and mobile probes of Globalping (`eyeball`) outrank it.
- **How it joins the verdict.** For each node the newest run counts as a live verdict: every tested port blocked, or some blocked and none open, is `tcp_blocked`; any open port makes it `ok`. Together with the other sources it follows [the rules below](#how-the-sources-join-the-verdict); the probe node is a path carrier there. A run older than three intervals no longer counts. The block lists stay detail only.
- **What is sent.** Commands to the probe node, over its agent link. The probe node then sends SYN packets to the addresses of your other online nodes (the TCP ports of their enabled profiles, 443 first; never to itself) and to two fixed addresses of the country: a control that is never blocked (Yandex) and a reference that the TSPU always drops, which is how the box is found. The references are built into the panel; when one cannot place the box, the panel tries the next, all three if needed. No node address goes to a third party.
- **No extra privileges.** The probe is an ordinary TCP connection attempt with a limited TTL, so the node agent keeps its hardened service: no raw sockets, no `CAP_NET_RAW`. It relies on the kernel handing the answers of routers to the connecting socket. That was tested on one kernel (Linux 6.6) only; watch the first run on a node with an older one, which ends as `no_dpi_hop` or `failed` if the kernel does not cooperate.
- **Only TCP.** UDP is not tested. A Hysteria2 or AWG node is judged by the TCP ports of its other profiles (VLESS, the HTTPS site of Hysteria2); a node with only UDP profiles and no site gets no verdict from this source.
- **Schedule.** Every interval the panel sends the probe node a few commands in a row (6 to 8 pairs of host and port each; a blocked pair is checked again alone, so one command can take two minutes) and stores each answer as it arrives. The next run is due when the newest stored row is older than the interval, so restarting the panel does not start one. A busy probe node is asked again after 5 minutes; an offline probe node, or an agent without `country-probe/1`, skips the round. **Test** runs it now (at most once a minute; it can take a few minutes) and shows the status and the verdict of each node.
- **Status of the last run.** `ok`. `control_failed`: the control address is blocked behind the box on the probe node's network, so the method does not apply there and no verdict is made. `no_dpi_hop`: the box could not be placed; the card says why (next point). `busy`. `failed`: no answer, an agent that is too old, or a lost link.
- **When the box cannot be placed.** The panel tries all three references, one after the other, and asks each at most once per run, so a run that places nothing costs three commands. The card then says what it learned:
  - *No TSPU on the probe node's path* (`reason` is `refs_pass`): a blocked address answered, so blocked addresses open from that network and there is nothing to test. Many data centres in Russia have no TSPU on their path. Pick a probe node with another provider, or switch the probe off.
  - *Routers on the path do not answer* (`no_hops`): the route from the probe node is hidden, so the box cannot be placed and the method cannot work on that network. Pick another probe node.
  - *Could not find the TSPU on the path*: the agent did not say why, as an agent from before this field. Update the agent and run **Test** again.
- **Off means off.** With the source off no command is sent, neither on schedule nor by **Test**, and Health shows nothing from it. Stored rows stay hidden for 7 days. **Disconnect** switches it off and deletes the stored rows and the settings (the probe node, the interval, the last run).
- **Not on the Cloudflare edition yet.** A run takes minutes and the scheduled jobs of that edition have seconds, so the card says the source is unavailable there and nothing runs (see [Not available in this edition yet](../getting-started/install-edge.md#not-available-in-this-edition-yet)).

## cheburcheck

The fourth source asks volunteers' probes across Russia whether a connection to the address of a node gets through the national DPI box (the TSPU). It uses [cheburcheck](https://cheburcheck.ru), a free site; the method is the one of the [TSPU probe](#tspu-probe), run from other people's networks. It is an integration of its own, **off by default**, and works for Russia only: with another country selected the card says so and nothing is sent.

- **Where**: the *cheburcheck* card in **Reachability → Sources**. Its verdicts join the node's verdict there, in the *From Russia* column and in the node row (the lines *cheburcheck* and *Own probe*).
- **What it checks.** One thing per probe: whether a TCP SYN to the node's IPv4 address, port 443, gets past the TSPU box on that probe's path. A probe answers `ok` (it got past), `tspu_block` (it died at the box) or `uncertain` (it could not place the box: no verdict). The port is always 443, whatever ports your profiles use. A TSPU address block drops every port, so this is a fair test of "is the address blocked" for every node. A block of one port, a block of the VLESS fingerprint or the freeze of an open connection is invisible to it, and so is anything on UDP (Hysteria2, AWG) beyond the address.
- **Not end to end.** `ok` means the SYN got past the box, not that the node answered: a router just behind the box is enough, and a node whose agent is down still shows `ok`. So cheburcheck is a *path carrier* in the verdict ([below](#how-the-sources-join-the-verdict)): it can show a block, but it cannot overturn a full block that another source saw end to end.
- **Verdict per node.** From the probes that answered `ok` or `tspu_block`: `ok` when none is dark, `partial` when some are and others get past, `tcp_blocked` when all are dark (the source tests TCP only, so it never says `blocked`). `unknown` when fewer than two answered (one for the own probe), when no probe of the site was online (`no_probe`) or when the site did not finish in time (`timeout`).
- **Public probes and the own probe.** *Public probes* (on by default) are every volunteer probe the site has online: home and mobile networks, routers and some hosting, untagged. The site cannot filter them, so the panel groups the answers by probe. The *own probe* is optional: you run a cheburprobe of your own, say on a friend's router in Crimea, and paste its token; the panel then asks exactly that probe, and the site does not cache its answers.
- **Settings** (owner only; every change asks for a fresh confirmation): on or off, public probes on or off, the interval of the public probes (default 180 minutes, and never less: the site replays an answer it holds for 3 hours, which would count as a second measurement), the token of the own probe (write-only, sealed in the panel's vault, never shown again) and its interval (default 60 minutes, 30 minutes to 7 days). **Test** reads the site's status page and sends no address. **Disconnect** switches the source off and deletes the token and every stored result.
- **What is sent, and to whom.** To cheburcheck.ru, the IPv4 address of each online node as the target of a check, and nothing else: no port, no protocol, no node name, no user. The site keeps **every query with the caller's IP address and User-Agent**, and the User-Agent names Mistgate (`mistgate-panel`), so the site can tell that these addresses are Mistgate nodes checked from one panel. On a VPS the caller is the panel's own address; on the Cloudflare edition it is a Cloudflare address that other people share. The token of the own probe goes to cheburcheck.ru with the probe request, as that probe's credential. Switching on shows all of this first and asks you to agree.
- **Schedule and politeness.** One call to the site per tick: the panel takes the node and kind (public or own) whose newest row is the most overdue, asks for a check and reads the probe stream for up to 45 seconds (35 on the edge). At least 30 seconds pass between two calls (the site allows 5 probe runs a minute from one address; the panel uses at most 2), so a sweep of six nodes with both kinds takes about 6 minutes on a VPS. The VPS panel looks every 30 seconds whether a call is due; the Cloudflare edition does it from its once-a-minute cron, one call a minute.
- **Back-off.** On `429` the panel waits 2 minutes, doubling up to 30, and stores no row. When the site has switched address checks off (`400`), the card says *The site paused address checks* and the panel tries again in 6 hours. When the site does not know the own probe's token (`404`), own runs stop until you save a new one. An expired check is asked for again once. The answers that came before a timeout are kept.
- **Text from the site.** The region, provider and AS number of a probe come from the site; they are clipped, stripped of control characters and shown as data.
- **Limits.** It sees the address and the filtering box, from the networks of whoever runs a probe. Volunteer probes are untagged and a few misread the box, so one dark probe proves little: the [alert](#the-alert) needs the same probe dark twice while it reaches your other nodes.
- **Off means off.** With the source off no check is sent. Stored rows stay hidden and are deleted after 7 days.

## bsbord

The fifth source is paid. [bsbord](https://bsbord.com) keeps SIM-card probes, called *units*, in the networks of Russian mobile operators, Crimea included; the panel asks them to ping each node and to connect to one of its TCP ports. It is the only source that sees Crimea and the mobile networks under an operator's white list. It is an integration of its own, **off by default**, and works for Russia only: with another country selected the card says so and nothing is sent.

- **Where**: the *bsbord* card in **Reachability → Sources**; its line in the node rows is *bsbord*.
- **What it checks.** For every online node with an IPv4 address and a TCP port, each unit you picked pings the address and connects to one port: the node's TLS endpoint (the REALITY port of VLESS or the HTTPS site of Hysteria2) if it has one, else 443, else its lowest TCP port. A unit that validates TLS needs that port to answer TLS. A node with no IPv4 address or no TCP port is skipped, and its source line says why (`no_ipv4`, `no_tcp_port`).
- **Units.** A unit is one operator in one district, with or without the operator's white list; its key looks like `mts|krym|off`. You pick them on the card from the live list of units that can probe now, by district; two presets help, *All of Crimea* (the default pick) and *One per operator*. The keys are stored as picked, so the price stays what you saw. A unit that bsbord no longer lists comes back as skipped and is not retried.
- **Verdict per node.** A unit says *reach* when ICMP or TCP got through, and *dark* when the unit ran and both failed. A unit that checks the TLS certificate fails any REALITY port, so its failed TCP proves nothing: it is dark only when the node answers other units' pings and not its own, otherwise it gives *no clear answer*. A unit under the white list that gets nothing is *white list*, never dark: the white list cuts every foreign address, for every node alike, so it is listed apart; one that does reach the node counts as reach. A unit that did not run, could not resolve the address or reported an error gives *no clear answer*. The row is `ok` when no counted unit is dark, `partial` when some are and others reach, `blocked` when all are (bsbord has no `tcp_blocked`: ICMP and TCP both failed), `unknown` when none counts. ICMP passing while TCP fails is still *reach*, because a unit that checks the TLS certificate can fail a REALITY port for that reason; the TCP and TLS results are kept and shown.
- **Settings** (owner only; every change asks for a fresh confirmation): on or off, the key (a `bsk_live_` key made in your bsbord account; write-only, sealed in the vault, never shown again), the units, the interval (default 24 hours, 1 to 24) and the monthly cap. Nothing runs before a key and at least one unit are saved. **Test** reads your account and the unit list (plan, balance, how many units can probe) and sends no address. **Disconnect** switches the source off, cancels a round in flight and deletes the key and the units; this month's spend stays counted, and the stored rows stay hidden and are deleted after 7 days.
- **What is sent, and to whom.** To bsbord, the IPv4 address and one TCP port of each online node; its SIM probes connect to them. The key is your bsbord account: whoever holds it can spend your balance there. Switching on shows this, then the price of a round and about a month at the interval. Asking the price is free but already sends the addresses, and no paid round starts before you have seen the price.
- **Schedule.** A round every interval, the first within a minute after switch-on. One paid request at a time, at least a second apart: up to 10 nodes go in one request, more nodes mean more requests, one per tick. Before every paid request the panel asks for the free price of that very request (the preview).
- **Money.** bsbord counts credits, one credit is one kopeck (10,000 credits are 100 ₽); the card speaks roubles. bsbord does not publish prices, the preview prices each request. In October 2026 one node on four Crimea units cost 80 credits and three nodes 160: about 10 credits more per added node per unit. Six nodes on four units once a day come to about 280 credits a day, about 8,400 a month (about 85 ₽). Prices are bsbord's and can change.
- **The monthly cap** (default 100 ₽, a calendar month in UTC) stops the spending: when this month's spend plus the price of the next request would pass it, the request is not sent, and the card says *Monthly cap reached* until the 1st of the next month. Raising the cap, or any other save on the card, makes the next round try again (after a free price check); it stops again if the cap is still too low. The card shows the spend, what is held for a round under way and what is left, and warns when the estimate for a month is above the cap. A request counts at its price when the answer arrives, minus what bsbord refunded for a unit whose channel dropped; one under way holds its preview price; one that is lost or cancelled keeps its price counted.
- **Restart safety.** The request is stored with a fresh Idempotency-Key before it is sent. After a timeout (bsbord can take minutes; the panel cuts the connection after 100 seconds, 40 on the edge), a restart or a panel update, the panel sends the same request with the same key, so bsbord answers from the first run and charges once. A request without an answer for 23 hours is given up (*A paid round got no answer*) and its price stays counted.
- **Statuses on the card.** *A round is under way*; *Monthly cap reached*; *The bsbord balance is empty* (tried again in 6 hours, or at once after a save on the card); *Your bsbord plan does not allow this* (change the plan, then save on the card); *bsbord refused the key* (save a new one); *A paid round got no answer*; *bsbord asked to slow down* (the panel waits as asked); *bsbord does not answer* (tried again later); *No picked unit could probe* (their white-list mode changed: pick others); *No node to check*; *No unit picked*; *bsbord rejected the round*; *The round was cancelled* (the settings changed while it ran).
- **Limits.** Units are SIMs in the districts you picked: bsbord sees those operators in those places and nothing else. That makes it a narrow source: its rows count in the verdict and raise the partial alert, but it never opens the full alert by itself ([The alert](#the-alert)). It tests ICMP and TCP only, no UDP and no protocol on the port. Units go offline; the list and the prices are bsbord's.
- **Both editions.** The VPS panel looks every 30 seconds whether a round is due; the Cloudflare edition does it from its once-a-minute cron, one request a tick.

## kl!ck reports

The [kl!ck](https://github.com/vbu00/klick) VPN app (Windows, macOS) has a server check the person starts in its window: from their own home or mobile network it tells, per server of the subscription, a TSPU **freeze** (the server answers and the latency checks pass, but a download stalls after 1–64 KB), blocked TCP, a failed handshake or no answer, and whether the network's DNS lies. With **send to the panel** on in the app, the result comes to the panel that issued the subscription. It is a source of its own, **off by default**, and looks from wherever the people are, whatever the country of the page.

- **Where**: the *kl!ck users* card in **Reachability → Sources**: the switch, how many reports came in 7 days and when the newest did, and per node how many reports checked it, how many of them saw a freeze, blocked TCP or no connection, and the same by operator and by region. A report counts once per node, in every verdict one of the node's servers got. Changing the switch asks for a fresh confirmation; **Disconnect** switches it off and deletes every stored report.
- **How the app learns of it.** While the source is on, every subscription answer to an app carries `Probe-Report: 1`. Without it kl!ck sends nothing and shows no switch. The switch reaches the subscription answers within a minute.
- **What comes in.** `POST <the subscription link>/probe`, JSON. The app sends one at most every 10 minutes; the panel takes two at once and then one every 10 minutes per subscription (sooner gets `429` with `Retry-After`). The token in the link is the only authentication: an unknown link gets the decoy, like an unknown subscription link. The panel checks every field (version, ranges, the known words) and refuses anything else with `400`. It matches each server by host and port against the person's own subscription; a server that is not in it is dropped. It answers `200 {"accepted":N}`, or `403 probe_off` while the source is off.
- **What is stored.** For 7 days: the node, the protocol type and port, the verdict and what was measured, and the network as the app saw it (operator, AS number, country, region, kind of network; the operator and region as plain text: letters, digits and simple punctuation, so no link, address or markup is kept). Not the address the report came from (neither stored nor logged), no person, no device.
- **Not in the verdict yet.** The reports are shown on their card and through the MCP tool [`klick_reports`](../reference/mcp.md) (any token profile) only: they do not change a node's verdict, its lanes or the alert.

## How the sources join the verdict

Each node gets one verdict from the newest fresh row of every source that is on: `ok`, `partial` (some networks cannot reach it), `tcp_blocked` (TCP is cut while ICMP passes), `blocked`, `listed` or `unknown`.

- **Fresh.** A row counts while it is younger than three times its source's real interval: Globalping 45 minutes at the default (its real spacing, which the budget can stretch), the TSPU probe 6 hours, public cheburcheck 9 hours, the own probe 3 hours, bsbord 72 hours, each as you set it. An older row is ignored. A newer `unknown` row takes its source out of the verdict; it never falls back to an older answer of that source, and the tooltip shows it with its reason.
- **Any reach plus any dark is `partial`.** A source reaches when it says `ok` or `partial`, and is dark when it says `partial`, `tcp_blocked` or `blocked`. With no dark source the verdict is `ok`. With none that reaches it is `blocked` when a source that tests end to end (Globalping, bsbord) says `blocked`, else `tcp_blocked`.
- **Path carriers.** The TSPU probe and cheburcheck only show that the TSPU lets the SYN past; a router behind the box can answer for a dead node. So when a source that tests end to end sees a full block (`tcp_blocked` or `blocked`) and every source that reaches the node is a path carrier, the block stays the verdict, with the confidence `conflict`, and the tooltip says who saw what. A reach from Globalping or bsbord turns the same picture into `partial`.
- **Three Globalping and TSPU cases changed.** Before, any disagreement of the two showed the worse verdict with `conflict` ("better a false alarm than a miss"). That predates `partial`, and a partial alert is not a miss.

  | Globalping | TSPU probe | Before | Now |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | `ok` | `tcp_blocked` | `tcp_blocked`, `conflict` | `partial`, `dc_only` |
  | `partial` | `tcp_blocked` | `tcp_blocked`, `conflict` | `partial`, `eyeball` |
  | `blocked` | `tcp_blocked` | `blocked`, `conflict` | `blocked`, `eyeball` |

  The confidence is `eyeball` when Globalping's round had home or mobile probes, else `dc_only`. Unchanged: Globalping `tcp_blocked` or `blocked` with the TSPU probe `ok` stays that verdict with `conflict`, and Globalping `partial` with the TSPU probe `ok` stays `partial`.
- **Confidence.** `eyeball`: a source that looks from home or mobile networks (Globalping with such probes, cheburcheck, bsbord) took part; for `partial`, one of the dark ones is such. `dc_only`: only data-centre views (the TSPU probe, Globalping from data centres). `listed`: a block list alone. `conflict`: see path carriers.
- **Block lists** never make a live verdict. `listed` appears only while no live source has a fresh row, and a live verdict always wins.
- **The breakdown.** Under every verdict that has networks which cannot reach the node, the page lists the networks, units and probes of **all** the sources that took part, each tagged with its source and the age of its answer, in the groups *Cannot reach the node*, *Reach it*, *White list on (cuts every foreign address)* and *No clear answer*, inside each by region and network. Region names are the sources' own and are not merged. A long list collapses ("and 27 more"). The tooltip says what each source said, with its reason. MCP gives the same as `vantages` and `source_verdicts` ([`checks_results`](../reference/mcp.md)).

## The alert

The alert `country_blocked` has two forms, a full one and a partial one, each with a life of its own.

**Full: the node does not open from the country.**

- **Events.** Every row of every source that is on, except the `unknown` ones, is one event, newest first: a Globalping round, a run of the TSPU probe, a cheburcheck or bsbord row. The panel [combines](#how-the-sources-join-the-verdict) each event with the newest row of every other source at that moment. Two rows are paired only when both are fresh: the gap between them may not be longer than the smaller of the two sources' windows. A block list never counts.
- **The own-verdict filter.** An event counts only when its own row agrees with the direction. A *bad* event has its own row `tcp_blocked` or `blocked` and the combined verdict `tcp_blocked` or `blocked`. A *clear* event has its own row `ok` or `partial` and the combined verdict `ok` or `partial`. Any other event is skipped, like an `unknown` round. With Globalping alone this is the old rule of two rounds; it also stops a TSPU `ok` run from making Globalping's `tcp_blocked` count a second time.
- **Opens** after two counted events in a row that are bad, **closes** after two that are clear.
- **Country-wide sources.** For the alert, a full result becomes `partial` unless a country-wide source says it itself: Globalping, public cheburcheck or the TSPU probe. The narrow sources, which see a few networks or regions, are your own cheburcheck probe and bsbord (on Crimea only, say). Alone or several together they only ever raise the partial alert. The page may still show `blocked` for bsbord on Crimea alone; only the alert is held back.
- **Text.** The alert and its Telegram message name the sources that saw the block (`globalping`, `tspu`, `cheburcheck`, `cheburcheck_own`, `bsbord`), the confidence, the networks that were dark (an external source is named after the network, as `MTS (Crimea, bsbord)`) and the ports that the TSPU probe saw blocked. The title is *Node is unreachable from Russia*. The text says what is cut (*TCP is being cut*, with *ICMP passes* when Globalping saw it, or *Neither ICMP nor TCP gets through*), then *Seen by: …*, and *The sources disagree; the worse result is shown.* for `conflict`. One message when it opens and one when it closes.

**Partial: one network cannot reach the node while others can.** The same kind with the subject `partial` and severity warning.

- **Observations.** Each row gives each of its networks, units or probes one observation, kept under the key `source|network`: `globalping|AS8359`, `cheburcheck|probe:17`, `bsbord|mts|krym|off`, `tspu|<probe node>`. Keys never cross sources: a cheburcheck reach does not clear a bsbord dark, nor the other way round, because a SYN that got past the box says nothing about what a SIM gets back.
  - *R*: it reached the node by its own source's carrier (Globalping: TCP to the node's ports, or ICMP when it has none; the TSPU probe and cheburcheck: the SYN; bsbord: ICMP or TCP).
  - *D*: it is dark, the combined verdict at that row's time is `partial` (a full block is no evidence of one dark network), and **it reached another node of your fleet** at the same time (the cross-node rule). The same time means: Globalping, the same round; the TSPU probe and bsbord, the same run or request; cheburcheck, a row for another node taken within one interval. A network that reaches none of your nodes therefore raises no alert; the page names it instead. A fleet of one node never gets a partial alert.
  - A unit under the white list, a network without a clear answer and a Globalping data-centre probe give nothing.
- **Opens** when the same key has D in its two newest observations. A round that did not ask the network, and every `unknown` row, is no observation, so the rotation of Globalping's operators or an outage does not erase the evidence. **Closes** when no key is in that state, normally on the first R.
- **Several sources.** The networks of every alarmed key are listed, as `MTS (AS8359, Novosibirsk)`, `MTS (Crimea, bsbord)` or `Rostelecom (AS12389, Moscow, cheburcheck)`; the TSPU probe is named by its node, `de1 (tspu)`. The sources of those keys are named too.
- **Held** while a source named in the alert has an `unknown` newest row (the cap reached, a `429` hold, a site that is down, the Globalping limit), because an outage is no sign that the block lifted, and while the node is not connected. If the dark network leaves a source, the alert ends by itself once its rows drop out of that source's last ten.
- **A full block supersedes it.** When the full alert opens, or is held open, the partial one ends as `superseded`, with no Telegram message. When the full alert closes, the partial one opens again if the rules above hold.
- **Telegram.** The title is *Node is unreachable from some networks in Russia*, the text *Probes on MTS (AS8359, Novosibirsk) get nothing from it while they reach your other nodes.*, then *Seen by: …*. One message when it opens and one when it closes. A change in the list of networks updates the open alert without a new message; muting works as for any other alert.

## IPv6

Globalping is the only source that checks IPv6. The block lists, the TSPU probe, cheburcheck and bsbord look at the IPv4 address alone, and the kl!ck reports do not tell the two apart.

- **What is checked.** With the IPv6 switch of the [Globalping card](#globalping) on (the default), each node whose IPv6 works is measured by its IPv6 address as well, in a round of its own. The control of that round is a site reached over IPv6 (`ya.ru` for `RU`, `www.cloudflare.com` for any other country), so only probes that have IPv6 take part, and a probe without IPv6 never counts against a node. The IPv6 verdict is judged like the IPv4 one, from the IPv6 rounds alone, and it never changes the IPv4 verdict, its lanes or its alerts.
- **Which address.** The node's address when it is an IPv6 address, else the source address of its default IPv6 route, which the node agent reports. A node is measured only when the agent says that outbound IPv6 works: an address that the hoster does not route would read as blocked.
- **In the admin.** The verdict pill says *IPv4* and an *IPv6* pill follows it, in the same colours, with the same marks and tooltip. Globalping's line gets an *IPv6* lane right under it, over the same 7 days. Under the IPv4 summary, one line counts the nodes cut over IPv4 (on some networks, TCP or all of it) that IPv6 still reaches. With the switch off the page looks as it did before.
- **No IPv6 verdict.** A node that has none shows one quiet line in place of the IPv6 lane, with the reason:
  - `no_ipv6`: *The server has no IPv6* (no global IPv6 address).
  - `ipv6_broken`: *The server has an IPv6 address, but IPv6 does not work at the hoster*. Outbound IPv6 fails, so the node is not measured.
  - `agent_old`: *The node agent is too old to report IPv6: update it*.
  - `not_checked`: no report from the agent or no IPv6 round yet.
  - `no_probes`: Globalping has no IPv6 probe in the country right now. A round that fails for another reason (`rate_limited`, `source_error`, `timeout`, `too_few_probes`) is shown the same way.
- **Budget.** An IPv6 round costs what an IPv4 round does for the same nodes: (1 control + for each measured node 1 + its TCP ports) x probes. Six nodes with two TCP ports each make 19 tests per probe over IPv4; two of them with IPv6 add 7. The two rounds take turns and share the [budget](#globalping), so without a token the rounds come a little less often. Switch IPv6 off to keep the IPv4 rounds as they were.
- **Alerts.** An IPv4 `country_blocked` alert carries the parameter `ipv6`: `open`, `partial`, `blocked` or `unknown`, what the newest IPv6 round says of the node. With `open` the alert adds *Over IPv6 the node is reachable from there.* (in Telegram, *Reachable over IPv6.*); the parameter changes without a new message. Alerts about IPv6 itself (subjects `ipv6` and `ipv6/partial`, parameter `family=6`, tagged *IPv6* in the admin) are raised only for a node whose [IPv6 entry](../guide/nodes.md#ipv6-entry) is on: the people on it depend on it. They open and close like the IPv4 ones, on two rounds in a row. For every other node IPv6 shows on this page and in the `ipv6` parameter only. For a node with the entry the `ipv6` parameter of its IPv4 alert is `entry` when the IPv6 rounds reach it, and the alert adds *People with IPv6 connect through the IPv6 entry.* (in Telegram too); otherwise the IPv6 alert speaks for IPv6 and the parameter is empty.
- **The address with an entry.** While a node's IPv6 entry is on, the IPv6 round measures the entry's address, the one people connect to, before the node's own address and the uplink's. A node is still measured only while its agent says that outbound IPv6 works.
- **When IPv4 is cut and IPv6 is not.** A node whose IPv4 is blocked from the country while its IPv6 gets through is a case for the [IPv6 entry](../guide/nodes.md#ipv6-entry): the node's page offers it, and the entry's own check (from outside, by another node) comes before it is turned on. Nothing is turned on by itself.

If only some networks lose a node, a [relay](../guide/relays.md) through another of your nodes that those networks do reach can carry the node's servers to them. The relay's checks and the hints in its dialog use this page's verdicts and the TSPU probe.
